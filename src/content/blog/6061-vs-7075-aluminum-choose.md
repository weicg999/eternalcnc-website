Choosing between 6061 and 7075 aluminum is one of the most frequent decisions our engineers help customers make. Both alloys machine beautifully, but they are optimized for different jobs. Get it wrong and you either overpay for strength you don't need, or end up with a part that fails in service.

Here is the practical difference, without the datasheet noise.

## The short answer

| | 6061-T6 | 7075-T6 |
|---|---|---|
| **Tensile strength** | ~310 MPa | ~572 MPa |
| **Hardness** | 95 HB | 150 HB |
| **Machinability** | Excellent | Good (more tool wear) |
| **Corrosion resistance** | Excellent | Moderate — needs coating outdoors |
| **Cost** | Low | Higher (raw material + tooling) |
| **Best for** | Housings, fixtures, heat sinks, general parts | High-stress structural parts, mold tooling |

**Rule of thumb:** if you are not sure you need aerospace-grade strength, choose 6061. It is cheaper, easier to machine, and resists corrosion better. Reach for 7075 only when strength-to-weight genuinely drives the design.

## What each alloy is actually good at

**6061-T6 is the workhorse.** It has excellent machinability — our shop can hold tight tolerances with fewer passes and longer tool life. It welds well, anodizes to a clean, even finish, and handles salt air and humidity without complaint. That is why it dominates enclosures, brackets, heat sinks, robotics frames, and consumer-product housings.

**7075-T6 is the specialist.** It delivers roughly 80% more tensile strength than 6061 at nearly the same density, which is why the aerospace industry builds structural parts from it. But that strength comes with trade-offs: it is more abrasive to cut, trickier to weld, and more prone to stress-corrosion cracking in harsh environments unless it is protected by anodizing or plating.

## When to pick 7075 (and when not to)

Consider 7075 when your part must **carry a real load** — a robotic arm link, a mold base, a high-stress mounting bracket, a racing component — and every gram counts.

Skip it when:
- The part is cosmetic, protective, or lightly loaded (housings, covers, trim)
- The part will live outdoors or in a marine environment without coating
- You plan to weld it (7075 is notoriously difficult to weld well)
- Your budget is tight — 7075 raw stock costs more and machines slower

## A note on anodizing

6061 anodizes to a beautiful, uniform finish — the standard choice for visible consumer and medical parts. 7075 can also be anodized, but the alloy's copper content can tint the result darker or patchier depending on the bath. If color consistency matters for your part, 6061 is usually the safer call.
