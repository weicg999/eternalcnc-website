The question I hear most often is: "Can you make it faster?"

We say yes. But the moment we say it, the real work starts.

The word "fast" glues two completely different jobs together. One is responding. The other is forming. They should run at different speeds — in fact, opposite ones. Mix them up and you stay uncomfortable forever: dragging your feet where you should be quick, rushing where you should wait. Both sides lose.

## 1. Fast is two things, not one

What the customer means by fast and what the shop means by fast are rarely the same thing.

When a customer says fast, they mean: "I send it, you answer me right away." "I change a drawing, you give me a read the same day." That is **response**.

When the shop says fast, they mean: "When will this batch be done?" That is **forming**. A part goes from raw stock to finished piece through cutting, roughing, stress relief, finishing, inspection, and surface treatment. Each step has its own clock. You cannot squeeze it out.

The trouble starts when one sentence ties both kinds of fast together. Promise a fast response and it is easy to also promise a fast build without thinking. So messages fly back instantly while the parts never show up. Or the other way around: the parts come out beautifully, but three days go by with no reply, and the customer has already found the next supplier.

So I split time into two ledgers: **the response ledger, and the forming ledger**. Keep them separate and you finally know where to spend your effort.

## 2. The response ledger: fast means giving something solid

A fast response is not saying "sure, right away." That is not speed; that is passing the question back.

Real speed is giving the other side something they can use to plan their next move. A drawing arrives. Can we make it, roughly what does it cost, how long will it take — even if you cannot give firm numbers yet, say when you can. The customer does not want a promise. They want a coordinate they can land on.

We set ourselves a line: routine quote requests get a reply within two hours; if the schedule is tight, within one working day; if it genuinely needs calculating, within two working days. This is not about looking diligent. The customer has a chain waiting too — they need your reply to arrange their own plan. You slip half a day, their side stalls half a day.

**A slow response does not cost you on your ledger. It costs them on theirs.** They cannot wait, so they find someone who answers the same day. By the time you reply, the order is gone.

So speed on the response side is not about quick hands. It is about whether you dare make a call on the spot. And daring to make a call comes from having the process, material, and pricing groundwork already built. Without that groundwork, fast is just wild promising.

## 3. The forming ledger: slow is physics talking

The slow in forming is not laziness, and it is not a process designed to stall. It is material and time having their own tempers.

Take the most common case. After a thin-wall part is roughed out, internal stress has to release. Do not let it settle and go straight to final dimensions. It measures fine when it ships. Two days later it has warped on its own. Inspection did not miss it — it changed after delivery.

Another case is work that needs a controlled temperature. The thermal expansion of carbon steel is about twelve micrometres per metre per °C; stainless steel runs sixteen to seventeen. A hundred-millimetre steel part shifts a bit over one micrometre for every degree the shop temperature changes. If you need stability within a few micrometres and the shop temperature swings, your measurements drift. Temperature control is not fussiness. It is what makes measurement meaningful.

You cannot steal this time. Take it away and it does not disappear — it comes back somewhere else: as rework, as firefighting, as a phone call at midnight.

**Every minute saved on forming comes back with interest.**

## 4. Why we keep getting it backwards

An account this simple should be hard to get wrong. Yet around us, getting it backwards is the norm.

Two reasons.

**First: the fast side is visible; the slow side is not.**

When a customer waits for a reply, they are watching a clock they can see. Answer half a day late and they feel it at once. But the stress-relief step you skipped does not show up today. Three months later the part warps in their hands. The gap is too long, and nobody connects the two events. So everyone pushes on the visible end and nobody watches the invisible one.

**Second: nobody keeps a ledger for where effort goes.**

A person has only so much energy in a day. Spend it firefighting and it is not spent smoothing out the process. Spend it explaining the same thing again and it is not spent getting the work right. It is like money — once spent, it is spent, and no more appears. The difference is that someone records the money and nobody records the effort.

I have come to understand one thing: moving with the times does not mean speeding up everything. It means seeing clearly which job deserves speed and which must not be hurried, then shifting your effort over. However fast the tool, it cannot change the fact that a piece of stock needs to release its internal stress. What the tool can do is let us **reply faster and judge more accurately**, and give the saved time back to the steps that cannot be rushed.

## 5. Split the two and people stop fighting themselves

In our shop we now put it plainly: anything involving the customer gets answered the same day; anything involving the part waits through every step that needs waiting.

Those two sentences look ordinary. In practice they change a lot. With fast response, the customer trusts you with the next step. With steady forming, the part ships without crossed fingers. Nobody has to be torn between "hurry up" and "slow down" — be decisive when it is time to be fast, and at ease when it is time to wait.

Someone asks whether this makes us slow.

No. What holds a shop back has never been those few steps that need waiting. It is rework, firefighting, and customers who waited for a reply and left. Plug those leaks and speed takes care of itself.

In the end, fast and slow are not opposites. They are two different things. Keep separate accounts and you finally see where you actually spent your effort today.

## The one line

**Fast response is respect for the customer. Slow forming is confidence in the part. Count them separately and you can talk about real speed.**
