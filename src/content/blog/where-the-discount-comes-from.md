## 1. An idea almost nobody argues with

Among Chinese manufacturers, one sentence travels very widely:

**"Give the customer a lower price, and you have created the most value for them."**

In the short run, that is not wrong. Same goods, lower price — good, no argument.

But the sentence carries a premise: **the goods have to actually be the same goods.**

## 2. Conservation of energy: what you give away comes from somewhere

Physics has a law called conservation of energy. Energy is never created and never destroyed; it only changes form.

I will not claim it applies cleanly to business — it is not physics. But one part of it does: **you cannot give away margin out of nothing.**

You have a life to live. A company to keep running. A material supplier to pay, a finishing shop to pay, power and rent to pay.

What you did not collect does not disappear. It comes back from somewhere else:

A cheaper grade of material. One inspection step dropped. Rejects mixed in with good parts and shipped.

This is not because anyone sets out to do harm. It is that **pressure finds its own exit** — once a price is pushed below cost, every remaining cent has to be clawed back from some step, and the step easiest to claw from is always the one the customer cannot see today.

The customer will not notice for a while. But they notice eventually —

A screw will not go in. It goes in, but will not hold torque. It assembles fine, runs two months, and the hardness is gone.

By then the loss is not measured in the price difference. It is measured in orders of magnitude above it.

We wrote about this two days ago in [Why Quotes for the Same Part Differ](/knowledge/tech-blog/quote-difference-quality-cost/) — which steps the low price transfers cost into: inspection, gauges, handling rejects. We are not re-deriving that here. One conclusion stands: **whatever is saved on the quote is eventually paid by someone.**

## 3. But the harder question runs the other way

If a low price pushes cost onto the customer, then —

**Where does the extra money in a high price go?**

That question is far harder to answer. It cannot be answered with ethics, and it cannot be waved away with "we care a lot."

A high price can be just as lazy: no explanation, no investment, relying on the customer not knowing, relying on switching being too much trouble.

**If a sum of money cannot be pointed at, then the difference between it and a low price is only direction.** One pushes cost onto you; the other keeps profit for itself. Neither is value.

So we have a phrase internally that is not very polite: **if you cannot prove the premium is justified, you are the expensive version of a cheap shop.**

## 4. We can ask a fair price because we can point to where it goes

Three places.

**First, the process route.**

Setting up a part is not "more is better" and not "less is better." It is **just enough**:

Strengthen what needs strengthening — load-bearing features, mating surfaces, the process route for critical dimensions, stress relief after heavy stock removal.

Save what can be saved — non-mating surfaces, non-functional appearance, tolerances that are on the drawing but that assembly never touches.

**The optimal route is not the maximum specification.** Maximum is only the most expensive, not the best. Putting a just-enough route on every part is far harder than selling one standard premium package — but that is what the customer actually needs.

**Second, inspection.**

In [From Drawing to Cost](/knowledge/tech-blog/from-drawing-to-cost/) we published a cost breakdown for one sample part — a 6061 aluminum housing, 10 pieces: inspection is 5% of the quoted price, admin and profit 17%.

The 5% line is visible on the quote. What is invisible is what sits behind it: the Go/No-Go gauges, the micrometers, the calibration cycle, and the hours spent checking before anything is allowed to ship. It produces no part at all, and it decides whether the batch you receive can go straight onto the line.

**Third, what the profit is for. This is the hardest to verify, and the one most worth asking about.**

Inside that 17%, admin and profit are mixed together. Part of it becomes: standardized fixtures, program templates, gauge management and calibration, scheduling systems, depreciation and replacement of machines.

The output of those investments is not "a prettier shop." It is **fewer actual hours for the same job.**

Here is the sentence I most want to put down:

**A price cut you can sustain can only come from efficiency, never from compressing cost.**

The first gets steadier as it goes — the process really did get smoother, the same work takes less time.

The second gets more brittle as it goes — because what you cut is not cost, it is buffer. Once the buffer is gone, the next disturbance lands directly on the customer.

## 5. What it costs a customer to change suppliers is the most underrated number in this industry

Changing a supplier is never changing a quote.

It is re-auditing a factory, re-running samples, re-approving a first article, walking the engineering team through the requirements again, letting the line adapt to new incoming consistency; and when something goes wrong, rebuilding the judgment of "will this person handle it."

None of that appears on any quote. All of it is real money. And it lands on the two busiest people the customer has — engineering and purchasing.

So we treat **"you do not have to change suppliers"** as a deliverable. That is not a courtesy line; it is something we price in. The value of a supplier who stays on a customer's list is not how much cheaper this order was. It is that the next order does not start over.

## 6. Being able to carry them is what makes it long-term

Customers grow.

This year it is dozens of loose parts, samples needed fast. Next year it may be small batch. The year after, it may be capacity, systems, and a full set of inspection reports.

The supplier's capability has to grow with them. If it does not, the customer has to change people — and changing people has a cost, as the last section said.

So when we put money into machines, process and people, we are not aiming at a few more orders this year. We are aiming at **being here, and being able to take it, on the day the customer actually needs it.**

A supplier who can walk with a customer for two years, and one who can walk with them for ten, should not be priced the same.

## 7. So what does creating value actually mean

It is not letting the customer pay less. It is **letting the customer worry less, fret less, and have an easier time.**

That sounds like emotional value. It is actually the hardest cost line there is: the attention of a customer's engineering and purchasing people is finite.

A buyer who spends every week arguing with suppliers, and a buyer who calls once a year about a price increase — whose company is more likely to build something good? You do not need to think about it.

Give them their attention back, and they can spend it on their own product.

That, as we understand it, is the most value a machine shop can create for a customer.

## One line for you

**Pushing cost onto someone else and keeping profit for yourself are both not value. Value is this — you can say where every extra dollar went.**
