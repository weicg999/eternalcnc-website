A shop owner complained: the government sent a folder on fire and safety management. Dozens of forms inside. Enough to keep two or three people busy for two or three days.

That is real. But spend ten minutes on the structure first and the workload looks smaller than it does — **those dozens of forms are often the same facts split into different formats.**

This article covers three things: what the statutory list actually requires, why the local checklist looks so long, and how to keep records so inspection day is not overtime.

## First: are you a “key fire safety unit”?

You do not declare this yourself. The local fire and rescue authority determines it.

The *Provisions on the Fire Safety Management of Organizations, Groups, Enterprises and Institutions* (Ministry of Public Security Order No. 61), Article 13, lists the scope. For machining shops, two categories matter most: labor-intensive production such as garment and shoe making, and “other units with a higher fire risk or where a fire could cause major casualties or property loss.”

Whether a precision CNC shop falls in that scope depends on the local authority's determination and your own fire risk. Don't prepare for the strictest tier on a guess. Don't assume you are exempt either. Confirm your status first, then you know which file set applies.

## The statutory list: two parts

Article 41 says a key unit's fire file has two parts: **basic information** and **management records**.

Basic information is static — site profile, key fire-risk areas, building acceptance documents, responsible persons, fire rules, equipment inventory, emergency plan. Build it once, update when things change.

Management records are dynamic. Article 43 lists nine items: official documents, equipment inspection and maintenance, hazard correction, fire checks and patrols, electrical testing, training, drills, fire incidents, and rewards or penalties.

Article 43 adds a detail often missed. For checks, hazards, patrols and electrical items, the record must state **who checked, when, where, what was checked, what hazard was found and what was done about it**. Training records need the time and attendees. Drill records need time, place, content, departments and people.

Those six elements are the yardstick for whether a record is adequate. A form filled in at the last minute is exactly the one that misses them — “checked, normal” with no person, time or location is no record at all.

## Why the local list looks like dozens of items

Local fire authorities publish a “materials to prepare for inspection” list. It often splits policy and record categories into a dozen or twenty lines. Policy items cover duties, training, patrols, evacuation, duty shifts, equipment maintenance, hazard correction, hot work, flammables, drills and reviews. Record items cover checks, patrols, duty logs, corrections, drill materials, training records, certificates, maintenance, annual testing, assessment reports and hot-work permits.

Daunting. But set it against Order No. 61 and the pattern shows: **the dozen policy items are Article 42(5), “fire safety rules,” written out; the dozen record items are the nine items of Article 43 divided differently.** Add a few local extras such as hood-duct cleaning or construction-site fire control and you reach that length.

So when the folder arrives, don't start filling. Sort the forms into “basic information” and “management records.” Once sorted, the real gaps are usually a handful.

## Five things to do now so records exist later

Whether those forms can be filled in a day depends on whether the traces exist. Recollection and last-minute writing never keep up.

**Log patrols the same day.** The six elements are person, time, location, content, hazard, action. Same-day entry takes two minutes. Reconstructing thirty days at month end takes two days.

**Close the loop on hazards.** What was found, who owns it, when it was fixed, and the recheck. A line that stops at “found a problem” counts as unfinished.

**Keep sign-in sheets and photos for training and drills.** Article 43 requires time, place, content and attendees. A sign-in sheet plus an on-site photo is the cheapest evidence, captured in the moment.

**Keep third-party reports for maintenance and testing.** Periodic equipment inspection, annual testing of automatic systems and maintenance usually produce a report from a third party. File reports by year. They beat ten pages of your own description.

**Store everything in one place, file by month.** Article 41 requires central storage for review. Records scattered across drawers take half a day to gather; records in one cabinet or shared folder take minutes.

None of these five needs two or three days set aside. They are two-minute actions folded into routine. Together they buy calm on inspection day.

## In one line

Dozens of forms are not dozens of tasks. They are dozens of ways of asking for the same facts. Know the structure of the statutory list, confirm your unit status, and log patrols, hazards, training and maintenance as you go. On inspection day you open the file and pull materials — you don't put the whole shop on overtime.
