> Industry watch · November 2026

Two meetings land in Shenzhen this November.

On the 17th and 18th, the APEC CEO Summit. Its theme is eight characters in Chinese: **共促繁荣，共赢未来** — jointly promoting prosperity, pursuing a win-win future. Hosted by the China Council for the Promotion of International Trade, with about a thousand business and government representatives from APEC economies invited.

On the 18th and 19th, the 33rd APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting. This is the third time China has hosted APEC — the first two were Shanghai in 2001 and Beijing in 2014. The overall theme this year is "Building an Asia-Pacific Community, Promoting Common Prosperity," with three priority areas: openness, innovation, and cooperation.

One more comes first: the fourth ABAC meeting on the 14th through 16th, also in Shenzhen.

I am not attending any of them. I just saw the words "win-win future" and felt there was something worth saying — because in our factory that phrase is not a slogan. It is an account that has to be settled.

---

## 1. Our version of win-win sits on three very concrete things

Saying "win-win" is easy. In a workshop it has a specific shape.

**With employees, it means people dare to speak.**

The company belongs to everyone in it. I have always believed that if a person has to work out every morning whether something is safe to say, and what happens if they say it, then half their brain is already gone. So on the floor I try to keep things transparent — who did what, how far it got, where the problem is. All of it out in the open. If you got something wrong, explain it. You do not have to guess first who is going to get blamed.

When the environment is easier, people stop thinking "how do I avoid a mistake" and start thinking "how do I make this better." Those two are very far apart. The first only holds the floor. The second is where ideas come from. And whether a person can go home to a comfortable life depends a great deal on whether those eight hours were spent braced or not.

**With customers, it means getting the hard thing solved.**

When a customer comes to us, it is usually not because they have a standard part that needs making — plenty of shops will take that. They come because the drawing is a little difficult, the batch is small, and the deadline is tight. When we take that work, the first thing is not the price. The first thing is whether it can be made, how, and where it will go wrong.

A quotation looks like one line of price. Behind it are dozens of judgments. We say we can do what we can do, and we say so when we cannot. We do not take work on a gamble. What we take, we find a way to make.

**With suppliers, it means paying on time.**

This one is the plainest and the most often ignored: **we do not stretch payment terms.**

A supplier also has wages to pay and rent to cover. If you hold their money for three months, they have to borrow to keep the material moving, and that cost comes back to your quotation one way or another. So paying on time looks like we gave something up in the short run. In the long run it lowers the cost of the whole chain.

None of these three is noble. They are just doing what ought to be done. But they share one property: **none of them settles in a single transaction. They are all long accounts.**

---

## 2. Win-win is not only "I will be good to you" — it is also "there is money I will not take"

This matters more than the three above, and fewer people say it.

As a decision rule, win-win does its real work not when it decides what to do, but when it decides what to refuse.

Some orders are priced very low, in large volume, and would fill the floor. On paper it looks great. But it crowds out every normal job on that line, and after a while the customer assumes that is your price level. Money like that comes in, and the account is in the red.

Some jobs are plainly beyond us. Take them anyway, and in the end you cannot deliver something that passes. What you have spent is the customer's time and your own reputation. That money is worse.

Some customers open with "can you do another five percent off" and never mention process, lead time, or consistency. There is no room for win-win in that relationship — only one-way price pressure. We would rather not have that order.

So in our shop, win-win is a ruler: **can this relationship keep going, can both sides feel the account works.** If it does not work, better not to start.

I think the state putting "win-win future" into the APEC theme is the same reasoning at a much larger scale. The twenty-one APEC economies account for roughly sixty percent of global GDP and close to half of world trade. The supply chains in this region are already inside one another — a single component may cross three borders before it goes into one machine. In a structure like that, whoever tries to gain unilaterally will not get far. Decoupling, breaking chains, raising tariffs — in the short run someone wins; in the long run the table gets flipped.

**Win-win is not morality. It is the only algorithm that keeps running.**

## 3. What these meetings mean for us, here in Shenzhen

I want to be concrete in this section, not rhetorical.

**First, the name Shenzhen will carry more weight in Asia-Pacific business circles for years.**

The meeting itself lasts days, but what it leaves has a tail. In the first quarter of this year, the Shenzhen Foreign Affairs Office received 75 delegations from 40 countries; the United States sent the most, focused on technology. For the CEO Summit, the US is currently the economy with the largest number of registered companies — Amazon, Walmart, Apple, Google, ExxonMobil, and Microsoft have all submitted applications, spanning semiconductors, AI, biopharma, energy, finance, logistics, chemicals, aerospace, manufacturing, and more.

For a small shop doing small-batch precision parts, this means one thing: when an overseas customer searches for CNC machining in China, the place name Shenzhen will carry a bit more trust. That is not an order. It is **a lower threshold to being seen**.

**Second, the agenda lines up with the work we want to be taking.**

The CEO Summit agenda: Asia-Pacific free trade area, connectivity, artificial intelligence, the digital economy, Greater Bay Area development, sustainable development.

Translated into workshop language: AI needs compute, compute needs cooling and structural hardware; the digital economy needs equipment, equipment needs precision parts; the Greater Bay Area is the Shenzhen–Dongguan–Huizhou manufacturing belt, and we are on it; sustainability will sooner or later become a carbon-footprint question from export customers.

Policy wind does not turn into orders directly, but it decides where capital goes and which supply chain heats up first. **When the wind is right, the money and the work downstream follow.**

**Third, the rules layer has the longest reach, and nobody watches it.**

Free trade area, connectivity, customs facilitation — these sound far from a workshop, but they land directly on our quotations.

How origin rules are recognized, how many days customs takes, whether certifications are mutually accepted — these decide how many days and how much money it takes for a shipment to go from our floor to the customer. A small shop cannot influence the rules, but when the rules change, our lead-time commitments and cost structure have to be recalculated. So these meetings are worth reading for the outcome documents, not just the headlines.

**Fourth, the green item will arrive faster than you expect.**

The EU's carbon border mechanism is already moving, and export customers will eventually ask: where did this material come from, where did the electricity come from, what is the carbon footprint. Few ask today. That does not mean nobody will. Getting your energy consumption and material origins straight early is money saved by starting early.

## 4. But let us be honest: a meeting is not an order

Having said all that, the other half needs saying too.

**In the short term, the first thing that hits us is traffic.**

On September 30 the Shenzhen Municipal People's Congress Standing Committee passed a decision authorizing the municipal government to set temporary administrative measures on public safety, urban management, and transportation during preparation and hosting, effective through November 30. On October 15 the city held a press conference on "green travel," encouraging residents to use private cars less or not at all and increasing public transit capacity.

How exactly controls will be applied will follow official notices. But for anyone in manufacturing the conclusion is plain: **shipments going out in mid-to-late November need to be scheduled earlier.** Inbound material, outbound delivery, customer plant visits — do not leave them to those days. This is not speculation; it belongs in production planning.

**In the medium term, what lands is rules and expectations, not contracts.**

When a summit ends, what is signed is a consensus document, not purchase orders. Real orders wait for the supply chain to move, and that is measured in quarters and years. Treating a meeting as a signal that "business is coming" is the easiest expectation to be disappointed by.

**In the long term, whether we can catch it still depends on our own hard skills.**

Whether customers come, and whether they stay, comes down to three things: is the precision steady, is the lead time accurate, is one batch the same as the next. Policy opens the door. Once you are inside, you stand on the work.

---

## 5. Back to that long ledger

The APEC theme is win-win. We have no ability to shape the rules, but every day we make a smaller version of the same choice:

Give employees an environment where they do not have to watch their back, or save that bit of management cost;

Take an order that looks good but wrecks your price position, or turn it down;

Push the supplier's payment terms out another month, or pay on time.

Every time, the short-term answer and the long-term answer point in opposite directions. **That is exactly why win-win is hard: it always makes you pay first and collect later.**

But after years of this, my experience is that the compounding is real. Employees stay. Customers hand you the difficult work. Suppliers think of you first when material is short. None of that shows up on a financial statement, but at the critical moment it decides whether you can deliver.

The state's ledger is much bigger than ours, but the arithmetic is the same. Twenty-one economies sitting down to talk about win-win are not negotiating who gives up what. They are working out **how to keep the table in place, so everyone can sit down to eat again next round.**

The meeting ends and everyone goes home. The table has to stay occupied.
