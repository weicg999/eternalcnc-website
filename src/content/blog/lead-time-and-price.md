## 1. One drawing out, three quotes back

You have a drawing. You send it to three shops and three quotes come back.

**The most expensive one is three times the cheapest.**

Most people react the same way: the middle one looks sensible, the cheap one is worth a conversation, and the expensive one — what exactly is he charging for?

So you call the cheap one. He seems straight. The shop looks like a real shop. The conversation goes well. You place the order.

**Then you start chasing.**

You ask in week three. You ask again in week four. In week five he says the material just arrived. Week six, the anodising is outsourced and the queue is tight. Week seven the parts land — twenty days later than you planned. Your assembly line sat idle for two weeks, and you've already apologised to your own customer twice.

**And now you look back at the expensive shop and it doesn't seem so expensive after all.**

That's what this piece is about. **A CNC quote only lists machine time, material and processing. So what is that shop actually selling that justifies three times the price?**

---

## 2. The line that never appears on a quote

Let's take a CNC quote apart. It usually looks like this:

| Line item | Can it be calculated? |
|---|---|
| Material (including scrap) | Yes. Grade, weight, market price |
| Machine time (at a shop rate) | Yes. Toolpaths, estimated cutting time |
| Programming and setup | Yes. Part complexity |
| Surface finishing (outsourced) | Yes. Process type, surface area |
| Inspection (CMM, first article) | Yes. Number of features |
| Overhead and margin | Yes. As a percentage |

All of that **can be calculated**. **Which means an honest quote should not vary by a factor of three.**

So where does the difference come from?

It comes from **this line — the one that never makes it onto the quote paper**:

> **Do I have to worry about this?**

That line can't be calculated, but it's real money. Its unit isn't currency. It's **your attention, your delivery promises, and the trust between you and your customer**. And it compounds — lose credibility once through a delay, and it takes three on-time deliveries to earn it back.

**The genuinely cheap option is never the lowest quote.** It's the one with the **lowest total cost** — once you add the chasing calls, the penalty clauses, the emergency re-sourcing, and the nights you don't sleep.

## 3. Lead time is about certainty, not speed

There's an easy confusion here.

**"Fast" and "reliable" are two different things.**

- One supplier **ships samples in 3 days**. But you have to chase every time. Replies are slow. Wednesday becomes Friday, Friday becomes Monday. He is fast — but **you cannot schedule around him.**
- Another ships **in 5 days**. He said Wednesday when he quoted. Tuesday afternoon it's in transit, Wednesday morning you have it, with a dimensional report attached. **You never had to ask.**

**The second is worth more, even though he's slower.**

Why? Because for the customer, lead time isn't about "what date it arrives". It's about **"can I put that date into my own plan"**.

Here's what a hardware company's schedule actually looks like: parts arrive → assembly → test → burn-in → certification → ship. **Every stage depends on the date of the one before it.** If you can't give a firm date, everything downstream floats, and the whole plan has to carry a fat safety buffer — **and a safety buffer is money: inventory sitting still, a line running below capacity, a launch pushed back.**

So:

> **The customer isn't buying speed. They're buying the ability to plan.**

**A date is a promise that can be kept. Speed is just a nice thing to say.** Which is why whether a shop dares to quote a real number matters far more than how fast it claims to be.

## 4. What is being one step early to market actually worth?

So far we've talked about saving worry. But lead time can create something more concrete: **a real window**.

Hardware runs on rhythms like these:

- **Fundraising.** Investors are waiting on the prototype. A month late with the prototype is a month late with the round — and another month of burn on the cash flow statement.
- **Trade shows.** The date is fixed. You cannot move it. Parts arriving two weeks late means showing up with last-generation hardware — and at that kind of event, one generation behind is **elimination on the spot**.
- **Customer evaluation windows.** An enterprise customer gives you a slot to prove yourself. The slot closes, and they move on.
- **Competitive position.** In the same category, whoever ships first sets the rules. Whoever ships second starts day one fighting on price.

**None of these windows wait.** And their value very often **far exceeds a three-times difference in machining cost**.

A rough illustration: say your product sells for over a thousand dollars, first batch of 500 units, and you launch a quarter late. The lost revenue, the dented channel confidence, and the extra marketing you spend clawing it back — **that number is an order of magnitude larger than three times your machining cost.**

**Saving money on machining is often trading a launch window for pocket change.**

Not that price doesn't matter. It's that **when you can hold a schedule with reasonable confidence, you can finish what the customer asked for inside their window, get the product into their hands, and let them launch one step earlier.** Customers understand the value of that — **nobody understands better than they do what time is worth.**

## 5. Holding a date proves the whole chain works

Let me turn this around and talk to my own side of the industry.

**A lead time isn't a concession to the customer. It's a health check on your own system.**

The lead time on a part is effectively the **product** of every link in the chain:

| Link | What breaks when it fails |
|---|---|
| Quoting and DFM review | Machinability missed at quote stage, surfaces on the machine three months later |
| Material purchasing | Hard to buy in small quantity, wrong grade, uncontrollable lead time |
| Programming and process | Unoptimised toolpaths, fixture not thought through, repeated trial cuts |
| Machine scheduling | Rush jobs, collisions, breakdowns |
| Surface finishing (outsourced) | The least controllable link: environmental restrictions, tight queues, rework |
| Inspection | CMM queue, slow reports |
| Logistics | Customs, flights, clearance |

**Look at the third from the bottom.** Surface finishing is the black hole in most machine shops' lead times — it must be outsourced, and the subcontractor's schedule isn't entirely yours to set. **Whether you can hold a date depends heavily on whether you've actually managed that outsourcing**: a stable partner, a fallback, and its cycle time built into the quote from the start.

So when a shop can consistently quote a real lead time, what it demonstrates isn't "how many machines it has". It's that **all seven links above are under control**.

**That isn't a reputation you can buy. It's one you accumulate.**

And the reverse holds too: if your lead times are always slipping, **the problem is almost never the machine**. It's a link somewhere upstream that has been quietly leaking for a long time.

## 6. What a reliable lead time saves on the customer's side

We've covered how a lead time gets built. Back to the customer: **what specifically do you avoid by choosing a supplier who hits dates?**

**One: you stop chasing.** The most direct saving. No progress spreadsheet, no messages every few days, no explaining to your own boss why it slipped again. **That time goes back into the product.**

**Two: you shrink your safety buffer.** If your supplier is unpredictable, you have to buffer everything — extra stock, earlier orders, slack everywhere in the plan. **A buffer is cash sitting still.** A supplier who keeps their word lets you take the buffer back.

**Three: you skip the emergency costs.** Firefighting is always more expensive than prevention: expedited air freight, a substitute supplier found in a hurry (usually pricier and less controlled), inspection relaxed to make the date. **Every fire eats your margin and your reputation.**

**Four, and most valuable: you avoid breaking your word downstream.** The date you promise your own customer rests on whether your supplier gives you a firm date. **If your upstream floats, your promise is empty.** In B2B, lost credibility is very hard to win back.
