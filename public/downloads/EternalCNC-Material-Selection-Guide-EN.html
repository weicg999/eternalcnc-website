Material Selection Guide: right choice beats expensive choice

Eternal CNC shop-floor notes · a quick reference for designers and buyers

Writing "aluminum" vs "6061-T6" on a drawing can change the quote by several times. Material decides three things: can it be machined, how long it takes, and how much it costs. This is not a metallurgy lecture — just what we live with on the machines every day: which materials cut easy, which cost more and why, and what to pick for what job.

2. Material by material (straight talk)

Aluminum 6061-T6 — the value king, pick it by default

For most structural parts, enclosures and brackets, 6061-T6 is enough. Cuts fast, easy on tools, nice surface — Ra 0.8 comes easy. If the function allows, this is our first recommendation. Don't second-guess it unless you need more strength or hardness.

Aluminum 7075-T6 — high strength, but tougher to chew than 6061

7075 is far stronger than 6061, used in aerospace and load-bearing parts. But it's gummier, builds up edge buildup, and wears tools faster — the 1.2× is just the material delta; real cost is a bit higher once you count machining. Not unusable, just "expensive for a reason."

Brass C360 — the free-machining champion

Cuts blazing fast, tools last forever, surface comes out bright. Great for precision small parts, fittings, decorative pieces. Only slightly above aluminum in cost, but the look is much better. First pick for brass connectors, gears, valve parts.

Stainless 304 / 316L — corrosion resistant, but "slow" is the killer

The real enemy of stainless isn't hardness, it's work hardening: a dull tool hardens it, and it gets harder the more you cut. You must run sharp fresh tools at high enough speed — no slow nudging. So short tool life and long cycle time are normal. Two notes: ① lead time runs 30–50% longer than aluminum, pad your schedule; ② loosen the tolerance one notch, and Ra 1.6 is the sane ceiling — don't insist on Ra 0.8. 316L resists chlorides better (coastal, chemical) than 304, but is slower and pricier.

Titanium Ti-6Al-4V — only if you must

Titanium's machining cost eats the margin: very slow, tools chip easily, and it burns (hot chips ignite). Reserved for biocompatible / high-temp parts (medical, aerospace). If 316L or 7075 can do the job functionally, switch — the savings are not small.

PEEK — for high-temp / insulating jobs only

PEEK handles heat, insulates, self-lubricates — the go-to for semiconductor and medical. Around 1.5× in cost, not slow to cut, but watch workholding (it lets go and deflects). For non-high-temp / non-insulating needs, plain nylon or ABS is far cheaper.

4. Old-hand reminders (save half your rework)

  • Write the full grade, not the family. "Aluminum" ≠ "6061-T6"; "stainless" ≠ "304". "Aluminum" forces us to ask back; "304" might lead us to stock 304 while you wanted 316. Just write the grade — 6061-T6, 304 SS, 7075 — and we're done in one step.
  • No verbal "material guarantee." Every order ships with a material cert (MTC, heat-number traceable). That's a real factory's floor, and what you should ask for when you audit a supplier.
  • Avoid recycled aluminum for critical parts. Cheap recycled bar carries more impurities and internal stress — easy to warp, strength unstable. Use prime billet for key parts; don't gamble a whole batch to save a little on material.
  • Want to resist rust and save money? Use a coating. Carbon steel powder-coated / zinc-plated, or anodized aluminum, is often cheaper than going straight stainless and reaches similar corrosion resistance. Consider surface treatment together with material choice.
  • No material on the drawing? Tick "as per drawing" or type the grade. In our RFQ form, material defaults to "as per drawing" — we follow what your drawing says; to switch or specify a grade, just type one line in the box.
One line to remember: if the function allows, pick 6061-T6; for corrosion resistance pick 304; for strength pick 7075; save titanium and PEEK for when they're truly needed. Right material = better cost, lead time and yield, all at once.
